Earlier this year the State passed a law
requiring HOA’s to allow shade structures in
backyards. The Architectural Review Com-
mittee (ARC) worked all summer to bring our Development
Standards into compliance with the new law. Over the last
three months they held seven public sessions, gathering
as much community feedback as possible. There have also
been two reviews by our attorneys. The revised Develop-
ment Standard approved by the Board is now on the Gov-
erning Documents web page on our SCOV web site. High-
lights of the changes are: Setbacks for Shade Structures will
follow the Town of Oro Valley requirements. View preserva-
tion cannot be the basis for rejecting a plan. Neighbors will
be notified but they cannot prevent approval of the plan. We
have established minimum material and construction stan-
dards and maximum size.

In summary, the recent change in the law does not elim-
inate the Association's architectural-review authority, but it
materially limits how that authority may be exercised with
respect to backyard shade structures. The Association may
continue requiring reasonable applications, regulating size,
placement, and appearance, verifying that structures are
commercially produced or professionally manufactured, and
enforcing safety and maintenance standards. It may not pro-
hibit a qualifying structure, impose height or setback require-
ments more restrictive than applicable Town zoning, require
neighbor approval, or rely on view preservation or architec-
tural compatibility as a substitute for a prohibited denial.

The ARC would like to thank everyone who took the time
to attend the meetings and reach out to us with comments.
With your help we have a better product.

This fall the ARC will be going through the Development
Standards to make some updates to points that are unclear
or in conflict with other parts of our governing documents.
We will be holding open meetings to allow for resident
comments during that process. Please follow This Week in
SCOV for announcements of those meetings.

Copper Center Update
Theda Ellis, 302-388-9798, tellisscov@gmail.com

tee ihe Copper Center al

The Copper Center was purchased in 2023
to help meet the growing need for space for
our artisan and other clubs. It is open for tem-
porary use, but it cannot fully serve our community until the
renovation is completed.

The Board is asking members to authorize spending for
the Copper Center renovation as funds become available.
This is not a request for an assessment, and it will not in-
crease your dues. The Copper Center's operating costs are
already covered by current dues. The Board is not asking
for a dues increase to cover operating costs or capital ex-
penses.

Why is approval needed?

Our bylaws limit the Board's authority to spend on capital
piojects to 10% of the prior year’s revenue—currently about
$1.3 million. Spending beyond that limit requires community
approval. Because the Copper Center renovation is estimat-
ed to cost $2,778,000, the Board needs members’ approval
to authorize spending above its current limit.

With that authority, the Board and management can plan
and authorize the work as funds become available, rather
than having to wait for another community vote.

Delay costs money

Construction costs continue to rise. Based on Phoenix-ar-
ea construction inflation rates, a project estimated at $2.7
million in 2023 would cost about $3.06 million today. The
prolest team has reduced the estimated cost by $331,000,

ut inflation is eroding those savings. The current estimate
is $2,778,000; if the work waits until funds are fully available,
the cost is projected to reach $3,215,900 by 2030. Moving
forward sooner could help limit further increases.

How could the work be planned?

Several approaches are being considered. One option
is to tackle the more expensive work first, while costs are
lower. That could mean relocating the artisan studios so the
Artisan Center can be remodeled at a more modest cost.
The Artisan Center work would be funded primarily through
Asset Reserve funds.

This approach could also prioritize groups with the great-
est space needs, including Fused/Stained Glass and Thim-
belena’s Sewing Club, while helping make room for other
groups that need more space, such as the Wood Shop and
Clay Club. The final sequence of work will depend on the
vote and available funds.

Spending authority is not a blank check
The authority would apply only to the Copper Center proj-
ect and only up to the amount approved by members. Funds
could not be redirected to other projects. The project would
use competitive bidding, with at least three bids sought
through a request for proposals (RFP). Regular monthly fi-
nancial updates would help keep members informed.

The bottom line
Approving spending authority would give the Board flexi-
bility to move forward as funds become available, help man-
age rising construction costs, and complete an investment
intended to serve our community for years to come.
Please attend upcoming Board forums and cast your vote
in support of this initiative.

UpKeep Helps Strengthen
Facilities Maintenance

SCOV's Facilities Maintenance team has
begun using UpKeep, an asset management
system designed to improve how we maintain,
track, and care for our community's facilities and equipment.

The team is actively responding to work orders through
the system, helping ensure maintenance requests are docu-
mented, prioritized and addressed efficiently. This improved
tracking provides greater visibility into outstanding work and
helps the team manage requests more effectively.

Beyond responding to immediate needs, UpKeep is help-
ing establish a more proactive approach to maintenance. By
developing preventative maintenance programs, the team
can schedule routine inspections and servicing to help iden-
tify potential issues before they become larger, more costly
problems. The system also provides a centralized location
for asset information, allowing staff to build accurate equip-
ment records, track maintenance history, and better under-
stand the condition of our facilities and equipment. These
efforts support SCOV's long-term goal of protecting commu-
nity assets, improving operational efficiency, and maintain-
ing our facilities for residents to enjoy for years to come.

Reasons Why Walking Is a Great Way To Exercise

Walking isn’t always thought of as a serious form of exer-
cise, But in fact, taking a walk is a better workout than most
people would expect. As a way to get or stay fit, walking
has some important things going for it, of which more people
should be aware. Here are some of the big advantages of
walking as a form of exercise.

It can be enjoyable. Walking is a lot more pleasant than
most other exercises, many of which can be downright mis-
erable. This is partly because walking can be done outside,
in nature. Experiencing the fresh air and natural sights of
the outdoors can be a wonderful experience, and numerous
trails and parks are perfect for walking.

It's low-impact. Walking is a relatively gentle workout,
especially in comparison to many other popular exercises.
Running, for example, applies significant pressure to the
joints and spine with every stride, while the movements re-
quired by walking place minimal stress on the body. This
means that walking will lower the risk of injuries.

Arizona summers can be brutal on the exterior of our
homes. Intense sun, heat, and monsoons take a toll on paint,
causing it to fade, crack and peel over time. Even paint that
looked great a few years ago may now be showing signs of
deterioration. Properly maintained paint helps protect exte-
rior surfaces from the elements, prevents extensive repairs
later and contributes to the overall value of your home.

Take a few minutes to inspect the exterior particularly in
areas that receive direct sunlight. Look for areas of fading,
peeling, cracking or bubbling. Upkeep of your home's exteri-
or paint is about more than just appearance. As homeowners
in Sun City Oro Valley, we all play a part in maintaining the
appearance of our community. Taking time to inspect your
home's paint and addressing areas that may need attention
now goes a long way towards keeping your home beautiful
and keeps our neighborhood looking its best.

The first part of November is when the golf
course has dried up from the water used during
overseeding. The ground is firm enough to allow
golf carts off the paths and onto the fairways.
This would be a good time to refresh everyone
on the rules associated with leaving the cart
path with a golf cart.

Many golfers seem to prefer driving down the entire length
of a hole on the grass. For those golfers that insist on driving
the length of the hole, there are “gates” where carts can pass
through to both leave and return to the cart path. Once the
cart leaves the path through the gate, it should drive straight
to the fairway. The cart should remain in the fairway until it
reaches the other end, where it should leave through the exit
gate. The goal of the gate system is to prevent carts from
driving the entire length of the hole in the roughs, creating a
worn path parallel to the fairway. This has happened in the
past, and must be avoided going forward.

That said, the 90-degree rule is always the preferred op-
tion instead of driving down the middle of the fairway. If a
drive ends up in the middle of the fairway, then you should
stay on the cart path and enter the fairway at a 90-degree
angle toward the ball. If the approach shot is near the green,
then exit the fairway at 90 degrees back to the cart path and
proceed to the green using the cart path.
