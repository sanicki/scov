The Sun City Singers - Why We Sing

By Dale

“Music builds a bridge, it can tear down a wall” is a great
truth. Those are lyrics from “Why We Sing” which the Sun
City Singers have performed several times over the past few
years, and if you come to the Spring concert on April 7 or
April 10 you will hear it again!

Sun City Singers was one of the earliest clubs to organize
in Sun City, and has regularly presented three concerts each
year. Over the years gifted SCOV residents have invested
their talents as singers, pianists, and directors. | recently
found a program and score to Handel's “Messiah” which the
Singers performed — with an orchestra! — in the late 1990's
including gowns for the ladies and tuxedos for the gents.
While we will not have an orchestra providing accompani-
ment (and no gowns or tuxes!), our Spring concert includes
some great music.

This year the April concert selections are drawn from the
history of America; it is our nation’s 250th birthday after all.
From songs of the early days of our fight for independence,
to ballads from the westward expansion, and somber piec-

Roskom

es about the price paid for our freedoms, the April program
aims to celebrate our country’s love of freedom and to honor
the ideals of our nation and those who have served.

Our 4th of July concerts are always chock full of patriotic
music, with call outs to the branches of service and recog-
nition of veterans along the way. Some of the singers, and
more than a couple in the audience, have been known to
wear their uniform for this event. And with free ice cream
following the singing, it makes for a wonderful afternoon.

The early December concerts reflect on the holiday sea-
son and always include a nice mix of sacred and secular
songs. Many in SCOV and the surrounding communities
consider that concert to be their kickoff to the holidays.

Tickets for the April 7 and April 10 concerts are still avail-
able for purchase in the Activity Center from Wednesday,
April 1st through Friday, April 3rd between 9.00am and noon.

As the song goes, “Music is a language that can speak to
one and all.” We would love to see you at our concerts!

Tucson Night Sky

Tucson, Arizona has some of the most
thoughtful night sky lighting requirements in
the country, designed to protect the region's
dark skies. These regulations limit the bright-
ness and direction of outdoor lighting, requiring fixtures to be
shielded so light is cast downward rather than into the sky.
The rules also favor lower-intensity bulbs and warmer color
temperatures to reduce glare and skyglow.

These measures are a good thing for several reasons.
They preserve Tucson’s spectacular night skies, making the
area a destination for astronomers, stargazers and tourists.
Dark skies support the thriving research institutions in the re-
gion, including world-renowned observatories. Environmen-
tally, reduced light pollution benefits nocturnal wildlife and
helps maintain natural behavior patterns. Additionally, lower,
well-directed lighting saves energy and reduces costs for
residents and businesses. Tucson's night sky requirements
strike a balance between safety, sustainability and preserv-
ing the wonder of the cosmos.

Residents are encouraged to review the HOA Develop-
ment Standards before installing or modifying exterior light-
ing to ensure compliance. By working together to follow
these guidelines, we help protect our community's views of
the stars. Then take a moment to step outside, look up and
enjoy Tucson’s beautiful night sky.

Paint: How Long Paint Lasts

After new exterior painting, the paint can generally last
anywhere from 6 to 12 years. Original painting variables in-
clude quality of the preparation work, how many coats were
applied, how it was applied and the quality of paint. Other
variables include exposure to sun (i.e., how much sun each
side of the house receives), severity of temperatures from
year to year, whether the paint has been patched over the
years causing variations in wear and color and if darker col-
ors arre used because they will fade much faster than lighter
colors. SCOV guidelines specify Dunn Edwards colors, but
not Dunn Edwards paint. Painters can and will use any paint
they want, which is why some general contractors will recom-
mend residents doing their due diligence in questioning what
kind of paint, etc. Dunn Edwards (and other paint manufac-
turers) offer several different grades of exterior paint and the
better the grade, the longer the paint will last. Using a con-
tractor who is licensed and insured is also recommended.
Check with the State of AZ Registrar of Contractors at roc.
az.gov. Signs that you need to paint: streaking, mottled look,
different colors indicating patch painting, grey or pink cast
coming through (which is the original stucco color showing
through the paint).

Architectural Inspection Group
Conveyance Coordinator Lisa Sandrock, 520-917-8072
Isandrock@suncityorovalley.com, Thursday, April 23, 2 pm

There is a group of individuals here in SCOV who quiet-
ly do their work without much notice or fanfare. Their work
goes on year-round, in the heat of our summers and the
cooler days of the winter months. They are meticulous in
detail and know every aspect of our current Development
Standards and Master Declaration. They stay current with
every change in those documents and other legal require-
ments pertaining to homes in an HOA. Without them, homes
cannot legally close escrow and transfer ownership.

The group is the Architectural Inspection Group, better
known as the AIG. Each year this group of seven or eight
individuals conducts approximately 200 listing and escrow
inspections as part of the closing on every home sold here
in SCOV. After each inspection, they file a report with the As-
sociation for processing. They do courtesy inspections when
requested by homeowners as well as over 100 re-inspec-
tions each year to confirm that violations found initially are
being addressed by our new homeowners in our community.

These individuals volunteer their time and talent to ensure
that SCOV remains a truly wonderful place to live. They de-
serve our thanks for the hours and commitment to that goal.
Thank you, AIG members, past and present.

April is the time of year when the peak golfing
season is slowly ending, the temperatures are

steadily rising and big changes begin to hap-

pen on the golf course. The golf clubs still have

major events in the first couple weeks of April,

so it is important to maintain turf conditions

at a high level, but with the warmer tempera-
tures and the chances of rain decreasing that becomes a
difficult challenge.

The roughs are starting to green up as they come out of
winter dormancy. As they begin to grow, the irrigation needs
to be turned back on as well. Throughout most of the win-
ter, the non-overseeded areas do not require irrigation but
instead rely on the winter rains. This keeps the course drier
and also saves on the water budget. Turning the irrigation
back on leads to more maintenance to ensure the grass is
getting the correct amount of water while trying to keep play-
ing conditions from getting too wet.

April also sees the start of transitioning away from the
overseeded winter ryegrass back to the underlying bermu-
dagrass on the tees and fairways. This is a challenge to
slowly remove the ryegrass while getting the bermudagrass
to grow and maintain good playing conditions for the club
events. This process is done slowly by lowering mowing
heights, verti-cutting the turf canopy to allow sunlight down
to the bermudagrass, aerification to alleviate compaction
and allow oxygen to the roots and small applications of her-
bicides to slowly remove the ryegrass.
