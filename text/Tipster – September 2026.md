What Does It Mean to Be a Fiduciary?

It's not uncommon for the Board to receive
letters reminding us of our fiduciary respon-
4 sibilities. The word fiduciary comes from the

Latin fiducia, meaning “trust.” At its heart, a fi-
duciary is someone entrusted to act for the benefit of others.

For a Board member, that means making informed deci-
sions in the best interests of the entire community, not for
personal gain or any individual group. It means following our
governing documents and applicable laws, avoiding con-
flicts of interest, and balancing today’s needs with tomor-
row's responsibilities to protect SCOV's facilities, finances,
and long-term vitality.

Fiduciary duty does not require unanimous agreement.
Reasonable people can review the same information and
reach different conclusions. The Board's responsibility is to
listen, ask questions, weigh the facts, and make the decision
it believes best serves the Association. Whether the issue is
maintenance, amenities, finances, or capital improvements,
every decision should be guided by one question: What best
serves the entire community, both today and for the future?

That is the essence of fiduciary responsibility and the com-
mitment your Board makes to every resident.

It Takes All of Us

Living in a community association means
more than simply owning a home, it means
sharing in the responsibility of preserving
something special. The quality of life we enjoy at Sun City
Oro Valley is the result of residents, volunteers, elected di-
rectors, and staff working together toward a common goal:
creating a welcoming, well-governed, and financially sound
community that you can be proud to call home.

Volunteerism has always been one of SCOV's greatest
strengths. Hundreds of residents generously share their
time, talents, and expertise to lead clubs, serve on commit-
tees, welcome new neighbors, support community events,
and assist in countless ways behind the scenes. Their contri-
butions enrich the lives of everyone in our community. If you
have ever considered getting involved, you are encouraged
to do so. Whether you volunteer for a single event or make
a longer-term commitment, your participation truly makes a
difference.

Strong communities also depend on strong governance.
While policies, procedures, and governing documents may
not always be exciting, they exist for an important reason.
They provide consistency, fairness, and accountability while
helping preserve the financial health of our association and
protect the property values of every homeowner. Good gov-
ernance is not about creating unnecessary rules, it is about
making thoughtful decisions that serve the best interests of
the entire community, both today and for years to come.

Of course, no community of nearly 2,500 homes will agree
on every issue, and that is perfectly healthy. Different per-
spectives often lead to better conversations and better de-
cisions. What matters is how we express those differences.
Respectful disagreement allows us to exchange ideas, ask
questions, and solve problems while maintaining the sense
of community that makes SCOV such a special place to live.
We may not always share the same opinion, but we do share
the same goal, a thriving community you are proud to call
home.

As summer comes to a close, we are pleased to welcome
back many of our seasonal residents. Your return brings
fresh energy to our clubs, committees, events, and neigh-
borhoods, and we look forward to seeing familiar faces
again. Whether you spent the summer here or are just arriv-
ing home, welcome back. Here’s to another wonderful sea-
son of friendship, involvement, and continued work together
to strengthen the exceptional community we all cherish. Be-
cause in the end, it truly takes all of us.

Protecting Employees

During Arizona’s Heat
Arizona's summer heat can create serious
risks for employees who work outdoors. New
state heat illness prevention requirements are
designed to reduce those risks by ensuring workers have
access to essential protections while on the job. Employers
are expected to provide readily accessible drinking water,
shaded or cooled areas for recovery, and encourage pre-
ventative cool-down breaks when needed. The proposed
standards also emphasize employee training, heat illness
prevention plans, and prompt recognition and response to
signs of heat-related illness.

At SCOV, the safety of our employees is always a priori-
ty. Our Facilities Maintenance, Common Area Maintenance,
Golf Maintenance, and other outdoor teams work diligently
to keep our community looking its best, even during the hot-
test months of the year. If you see employees taking a break
in the shade or stopping to hydrate, please know these prac-
tices help protect their health and ensure they can continue
working safely. A few minutes spent cooling down today can
prevent a serious medical emergency tomorrow.

Keep It Simple

Swimming and water aerobics work muscles many of
us do not use every day, so it is normal to tire sooner than
expected. Start with 10 to 20 minutes, alternating between
swimming, water aerobics, walking, and resting as needed.
Adults should aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate-in-
tensity activity each week. Whether you walk through the
water at the beach, take a water aerobics class, or swim a
few easy lengths, each dip in the water supports a healthier
routine. Water supports your weight, making it a low-impact
way to build strength, improve cardiovascular fitness, and
stay active at almost any age.

Protecting Our Community's Value Starts at Home

Keeping a home looking its best takes time, effort, and
ongoing care. Whether it's repainting your home, replacing
landscaping, installing new windows or doors, adding a patio
cover, or making other exterior improvements, every proj-
ect contributes to the overall appearance and value of our
neighborhood.

Before beginning any exterior project, please take a few
moments to contact the Architectural Review Committee
(ARC) to determine whether approval is required. The ARC
is here to help ensure that improvements are consistent with
our community standards and governing documents, not to
make projects more difficult.

Obtaining approval before work begins can also prevent
costly surprises down the road. When a home is sold, an
architectural inspection is performed to verify that exterior
modifications comply with community requirements. Proj-
ects completed without the necessary approval may require
additional documentation or corrective action before the sale
can move forward.

Alittle planning today can save time, expense, and frustra-
tion tomorrow. More importantly, it helps preserve the attrac-
tive appearance of our community and protects the property
values we all share.

Thank you for continuing to invest in your home and for
helping keep SCOV a community we are all proud to call
home.

September is the time of year when the golf

course is overseeded which converts the turf

from warm season bermudagrass to cool sea-

son ryegrass. Typically, the tees, fairways and

starting last year the aprons around the greens,

are overseeded to provide a playing surface that

remains green and growing during the winter

months when the bermudagrass turns brown in dormancy.

This year the greens will be overseeded for the first time

since they were rebuilt in 2020 with Tifeagle Ultradwarf Ber-
mudagrass.

The greens were not overseeded so the new turf could
mature and develop a deep root system over time. The neg-
atives of not overseeding the greens are old plugs from the
daily moving of cups not healing which start to impact put-
ting conditions. The non-overseeded turf also stops growing
when dormant, so it wears out unevenly causing poor condi-
tions combined with faster putting speeds.

Overseeding the greens will provide a good consistent
putting surface during the winter, and the old plugs will not
be a problem. However, overseeded greens have to be tran-
sitioned back to bermudagrass the following summer which
could lead to thin areas that either need repairing or time to
heal. Overseeding also leads to disease issues with both
the underlying bermudagrass and the overseeded turf which
requires the use of fungicides. Overseeding also requires
higher inputs of water and fertilizers which tends to cause
wetter playing conditions.

This year, the decision was made to provide the best pos-
sible playing conditions in the winter by overseeding the
greens.

NO POISON PLEASE!

What to do about those pesky rodents who want to gnaw
their way into your car wires or air conditioner. Here are
some tips for keeping them at bay — none of which involve
poison because bait boxes containing rodenticides are kill-
ing so very much more than just a rodent! Raptors, coyotes
and bob cats see an easy meal in a floundering or dead
poisoned rat. The poison in the rat will then poison the pred-
ator, leading to death, or in the case of a coyote or bob cat,
mange and then death.

So, what to do? Here are some tips if you have an “in-
festation” of rodents: Call around to the many pest compa-
nies and ask how they deal with the problem. If the company
says they put out bait boxes with rodenticide, hang up and
go on to the next company on Google. If you don’t want to
use a company, please don't run down to the local hardware
store and buy bait boxes filled with rodenticide. Instead, set
traps in the “infected” area. Here in Sun City we have sever-
al people who will come empty your traps if you need help.
Remember, there is no such thing as a safe poison!

Don't have an infestation, but you're worried it might hap-
pen? Here are some tips to keep those guys away:

Plants that pack rats love: Agave, prickly pear, yucca and
cholla. So plant these at your own risk.

Plants that pack rats despise: Lavender, rosemary, allums
and daffodils.

Scents that pack rats can’t stand: Peppermint, vinegar (so
if you do weed control with vinegar you are also controlling
the rats), ammonia and citronella. To use these scents, soak
cotton balls and place them in strategic locations like under
your car or tucked under your air conditioner.

Encourage owls to nest nearby. One family of barn owls
will eat 12-40 pack rats in one night.

Also, limb up plants, especially near your house. Clean up
bird feeders or just don't have one. Protect your downspouts
with stainless steel wire mesh ($3 at the hardware store),
plug up entryways in your air conditioner and roof vents, or
go to PACC and bring home a rat terrier.

Pack rats are absolutely vital to a healthy desert ecosys-
tem. They do the following good things for the desert: Aer-
ate the soil, dispense seeds, their poop helps fertilize plants,
and they are food for bobcats, coyotes, owls, hawks and
even snakes. Therefore, wholesale killing of these guys isn’t
the answer, but instead learning to live with them is the goal.
