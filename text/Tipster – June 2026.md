Wildlife in SCOV

By Mariana Corella & Laura Hackenbrock

As dwellers of the Sonoran Desert, we are all familiar with
the unique and beautiful wildlife we get to share our homes
with. We live amongst javelinas, coyotes, hummingbirds,
deer, rabbits, hawks, and dozens of other special animals. It
is important to remember to be kind and respecful of these
creatures. Some animal lovers in the community are work-
ing to make sure we all maintain a healthy relationship with
them.

As some of you may know, great horned owls have made
a nest close to the Gift Shop, and we have an update
brought to you by SCOV resident and AZ Raptor Center's
Laura Hackenbrock, “By now, you've probably heard the
heartwarming news, this spring, our SCOV Great Horned
Owl nest welcomed an unexpected addition. An orphaned
chick, rescued and in need of a family, was placed into the
nest through the Arizona Raptor Center’s Re-Nest program.
Volunteers search for active nests each year, and this sea-
son, ours became the chosen refuge.

The little owl arrived just after sunrise on April 10. Though
it was clearly younger, one to two weeks behind the resident
owlets, the family accepted it almost immediately. Within a
day, the newcomer had blended into the nest's daily rhythm
as if it had always belonged there.

Photos provided by Laura Hackenbrock

Mom kept a steady watch from a nearby branch, and on
windy days, she climbed into the nest to shield the chicks
with her body. Dad hunted through the night, returning with
meals that Mom carefully prepared, tearing prey into small
pieces for eager beaks. As the owlets grew, they quickly
learned to feed themselves from delivered food.

At about five weeks old, the youngsters began ‘branching,’
venturing out of the nest to test their balance on nearby limbs.
One day, an older owlet's first attempt at flight knocked the
nest loose, sending all the chicks onto surrounding branches
sooner than expected. Still, they adapted quickly, gripping
their new perches and watching the world from their elevat-
ed vantage points.

By seven weeks, they were taking short, tentative flights,
small but thrilling steps toward independence. Yet their time
with their parents is far from over. Great Horned Owl families
often remain together for up to nine months, with the young
continuing to receive food and guidance as they slowly learn
to hunt on their own.

Mark your calendar for Tuesday, November 10 when the
AZ Raptor Center will be making a special presentation in
SCOV. The program will be an exciting experience with rap-
tors and discussion about the Re-Nest program.”

General Manager - Sandy Seddon

Sandy Seddon sseddon@suncityorovalley.com 520-917-8080

If you have recently noticed a herd of goats
working along a portion of the wash, you are
not imagining things. The goats have officially
arrived at SCOV as part of a pilot project to
evaluate an alternative method for brush and
vegetation removal in our common areas.

As expected, the project has generated a variety of opin-
ions and some controversy. Some residents have expressed
concerns about the possible impact on local wildlife and the
natural habitat within the wash. Others believe utilizing goats
is a more environmentally friendly and natural approach
compared to traditional mechanical clearing methods.

What we do know, is that the goats are proving to be an
efficient tool in reducing dense brush and overgrowth. This
method may ultimately save staff time and manpower while
potentially providing a more effective way to maintain difficult
terrain along the wash and golf course areas.

SCOV is not alone in trying this approach. Several HOA
communities throughout Southern Arizona have successful-
ly used goats for common area cleanup and fuel reduction
projects, with many reporting positive results.

At this point, this remains an experiment and evaluation
process for SCOV. Staff and management will monitor the
effectiveness, cost, environmental impact, and overall re-
sults before determining whether this is something we would
continue in the future.

At the very least, we hope residents have enjoyed watch-
ing the goats hard at work as they have quickly become one
of the more talked-about visitors to the community. We also
appreciate everyone continuing to maintain a safe distance
from the animals and the fencing while the project is under-
way.

Goatscaping in SCOV

From approximately May to June 21, along

the washes, golf course edges, and common
areas, an unusual but highly effective land-
scape maintenance crew has been hard at
work. More than 180 goats and sheep from local herders at
Natures Surprise Ranch LLC Goat Grazing. Known as “Na-
ture's original weed wackers,” these hardworking animals
provide an environmentally friendly approach to vegetation
management while bringing plenty of personality to the land-
scape.
The herd is made up of several different breeds, each
contributing unique grazing abilities. Boers and Kikos are
hardy, muscular goats that tackle dense brush and rough
terrain with ease. Nubians and Alpines are excellent all-
around grazers that help clear medium-height vegetation,
while La Manchas and Oberhasli goats efficiently consume
leafy weeds and lower shrubs. Spanish goats are particular-
ly agile on rocky slopes and washes, and smaller Nigerian
Dwarfs clean up low-growing vegetation and hard-to-reach
areas. Savannas, known for their durability and strong appe-
tites, assist with broad vegetation control across all grazing
Zones.

Adding to the team are Dorper and Black Belly sheep,
breeds known for their ability to graze grasses efficiently.
Their inclusion helps create a balanced landscaping ap-
proach by addressing vegetation types that goats may by-
pass. Together, the herd naturally clears overgrowth at mul-
tiple levels — low, medium and high — creating a thorough
and sustainable maintenance process.

This goatscaping session has even welcomed new arriv-
als, with several baby goats born while on site, delighting
observers and adding to the charm of the project. Protecting
and guiding the herd are two dedicated dogs. One is a Kan-
gal and Great Pyrenees mix that remains with the animals
overnight as their guardian against nocturnal predators. The
second dog, Orian, works alongside the herders during the
day, helping move the herd safely and efficiently from one
grazing location to the next before returning with the team
each evening. While the herders supervise the herd during
the day, temporary electric fencing keeps the animals safely
contained and is moved every one to three days depending
on the desired level of vegetation clearing.

Through this innovative process, Natures Surprise demon-
strates how natural grazing can provide effective, cost-con-
scious and eco-friendly landscape maintenance. In many
areas, the herd can clear vegetation in approximately six
weeks, work that could otherwise take maintenance crews
up to three months to complete using traditional methods.
As a pilot program, the project also provides an opportunity
to evaluate long-term benefits such as improved vegetation
management, reduced fuel usage and a more sustainable
approach to maintaining washes, golf course perimeters and
common areas.

Match Your Movement to How You Feel

Some days the workout you planned just doesn’t match
where your body is at. Maybe you have planned an after-
noon run, but after a day of back-to-back meetings going to
the doctor and having little sleep, it feels impossible. But that
doesn’t mean you skip it. The goal of a routine isn’t to do the
same workout no matter what, it’s to keep showing up. And
some days, showing up looks different. Our energy naturally
shifts from day to day. So instead of forcing a workout that
doesn't fit or skipping movement altogether, adjust it. This is
a habit you can come back to each week. Check in with how
you feel, then choose your movement.

As summer approaches, whether you're at home or away,
it's important to keep in mind your responsibilities as a
homeowner. Weeds growing in driveways, sidewalks and

blocking the visibility of stop signs. Additionally, any exposed
soil on your property should be covered with rock. If you

as artificial ones are prohibited. Lastly, for safety, make sure

which should be well-lit and visible from the street at all
times. Thank you for your attention to these reminders and
enjoy a wonderful summer.

mkropf@suncityorovalley.com 5:
ine is a very slow time for many of the ac-
ti S around the community. However, the
summer months start the busy season for golf
course maintenance. During the winter months
there aren't many opportunities to aerify greens,
aerify and verticut fairways or do any major proj-
ects without interrupting tournaments or impact-
ing play during the peak golfing season.

Even though the heat of summer is miserable for golfers,
the bermudagrass thrives with these higher temperatures.
The higher soil temperatures provide the best time to aerify
greens, tees and fairways because they are able to recover
quickly without disturbing as much golf. Aerification is nec-
essary to provide oxygen, water and nutrients to the roots,
remove thatch and relieve compaction developed over a
season of heavy golf and cart traffic. This process provides
a healthier and more playable turf in the long run.

Ever since it was announced the greens will be overseed-
ed this fall there has been some confusion on what type of
grass the greens will be overseeded with. The goal is to pro-
vide a good putting surface while also being less harmful
to the underlying bermudagrass so a combination of Poa
Trivialis and creeping bent grass will be used. Poa Trivialis
or rough bluegrass, is not the same as Poa Annua or Annual
Bluegrass. Poa Annua is the small, patchy weed with lots of
seed heads that can be found on the greens and fairways.
Poa Trivialis provides a smooth putting surface, doesn’t
have seed heads and has a darker green color.
